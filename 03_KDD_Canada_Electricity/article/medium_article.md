# Is Canada's Grid Getting Less Dependent on Burning Fuel? What 18 Years of StatCan Data Actually Say

Ask most people whether Canada's electricity is getting cleaner and they'll say yes: coal plants are closing and wind farms are going up. I wanted to check that against the data. Statistics Canada publishes monthly generation by fuel type and electricity trade for every province and territory, and a Kaggle user packaged 18 years of it.

The short answer surprised me. **Nationally, the share of electricity generated by burning fuel was 21.2% in 2016 and 22.9% in 2025.** It didn't fall. But that flat line hides two provinces moving fast in opposite directions, and finding that out is what the KDD process is for.

## Why KDD, not "just do some data science"

The KDD process (Fayyad, Piatetsky-Shapiro & Smyth, 1996) describes the path from a database to *knowledge* as **selection → preprocessing → transformation → data mining → interpretation/evaluation**, with the domain goals first and the use of the knowledge last. Two ideas in it shaped this project:

1. **Most of the work is before the mining.** Raw government tables have nested subtotals, suppression codes, two tables that must agree, and a hidden break in how the numbers were collected.
2. **A pattern isn't knowledge until it's validated.** Fayyad et al. call this "interestingness": validity, novelty, usefulness and understandability. I turned validity into pass/fail rules and wrote them down *before* mining.

## Step 0: four questions, and how each could fail

| Goal | Question | Fails if… |
|---|---|---|
| K1 | Which provinces have similar power systems? | the 2016–24 groups don't reappear in 2025–26 (adjusted Rand index < 0.7) |
| K2 | When did each province's combustion share change? | fewer than 3 provinces (besides Ontario) have a change that is stable and still there in 2025–26 |
| K3 | Which months were anomalous? | flagged months don't match a list of public-record events (storms, cold snaps, droughts…) better than chance |
| K4 | Can we forecast demand better than "same month last year"? | the error isn't at least 10% lower on two forecast years kept sealed until the end |

Everything from January 2025 on was **sealed**: the data loaders refuse to return it unless explicitly asked, and unit tests check that. The rules, and a hash of every file that defines them, were frozen before any mining ran.

## Steps 1–3: where the real work was

**A break nobody mentions.** From 2015 to 2016 Canada's total reported generation jumps 9% and wind 3.4×. Electricity didn't change; the survey did. Statistics Canada started counting industrial wind, solar and tidal producers and reclassified thermal plants by fuel. Any trend that crosses 2016 compares two different surveys, so every analysis here starts in 2016.

**Cleaning:**
- Missing values are almost all *structural*: Yukon publishes no trade because it has no grid connection, and there's no nuclear outside Ontario and New Brunswick. Those are zeros, not gaps to fill.
- The two StatCan tables agree on total generation exactly, in all 1,404 province-months.
- Generation + imports − exports = availability holds everywhere except one BC month, which I flagged instead of "fixing".

**Two smaller breaks** turned up the same way:
- Nunavut's generation doubles in October 2024 because mines start being counted;
- Newfoundland adds industrial hydro in February 2021.

Both were registered before mining. (The critic found the second one; more on the critic below.)

**Transformation:**
- Generation mix as shares;
- trade as a fraction of what each province uses;
- a "typical year" seasonal profile;
- de-seasonalized combustion shares;
- STL residuals for anomaly detection.

## Step 4: data mining

**K1, clustering.** k-means and hierarchical (Ward) clustering on eight features agree exactly. The silhouette score picks five groups:
- **hydro:** Quebec, Manitoba, BC, Yukon;
- **combustion:** Alberta, Saskatchewan, Nova Scotia, NWT, Nunavut;
- **nuclear-mixed traders:** Ontario, New Brunswick;
- **PEI on its own:** all wind, and it imports more than half its electricity;
- **Newfoundland and Labrador on its own:** it exports nearly three times what it uses (Churchill Falls).

**K2, change points.** PELT (via `ruptures`) on each province's de-seasonalized combustion share. Two results stand out:
- **Alberta:** its share falls from 2023. Combustion is down about 270 GWh a month and wind + solar up about 365. That's *less burning*, not just more total generation.
- **Ontario:** the opposite. From mid-2023 combustion is up about 900 GWh a month, with demand and exports both up.

**K3, anomalies.** An Isolation Forest scores each province-month on its STL residuals and flags the top 2% (28 months).

**K4, forecasting.** Averaging a growth-adjusted 3-year seasonal mean with Holt-Winters was 12% better than seasonal naive on six development years.

## Step 5: opening the seal

| Goal | Result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| K1 archetypes | All 13 provinces and territories in the same group in 2025–26 (ARI 1.00) | ✅ Met |
| K2 structural change | 5 provinces qualify by the rule; in substance, Alberta and Nova Scotia (plus a small Newfoundland decline) | ✅ Met as written |
| K3 anomalies | 3 of 28 flags matched an event; no better than chance (p = 0.25) | ❌ Not met |
| K4 forecasting | Error ratio 1.015: slightly *worse* than seasonal naive | ❌ Not met |

**What the passes mean.**
- **K1:** the grouping is real and stable at five groups (at three or four groups it isn't), but it's not news. Anyone who has read the Canada Energy Regulator's provincial profiles would sketch the same groups.
- **K2:** the interesting result. **Alberta** went from 91.5% combustion in 2016 to 78.2% in 2024, 76.0% in 2025 and 72.4% so far in 2026. **Nova Scotia** fell from about 81% to about 74%. **Ontario** went from under 9% in 2017–20 to 17.5% in 2024 and 21% in 2025. Roughly half of that is gas replacing nuclear output lost to refurbishments; the rest is demand growth and exports. Ontario's extra 18.6 TWh of burning outweighs Alberta's 6.7 TWh cut. The 2023–24 droughts that cut hydro output in Quebec, BC and Manitoba shrank the total. Add those together and the national share goes nowhere. About 40% of the national rise is just the smaller total.
- **Fine print on K2 (the critic caught this):** two of the five provinces (PEI, BC) "qualified" only because a temporary 12-month bump ended. BC has no net decline at all. My rule measured the largest jump between neighbouring segments, not the change from the start. The rule said "met", and I report it as met, but I also show a labelled after-the-fact check (net change ≥ 1 point and driven by burning less). Only Alberta, Nova Scotia and a small Newfoundland decline survive it, exactly the three needed.

**What the failures taught me.**
- **K3** failed: 3 of 28 flags matched an event, about what random placement gives. Monthly totals smooth away a one-day ice storm. My first story was "the flags are nuclear outages", and the critic checked it against my own data: only 4 of New Brunswick's 11 flags were outages. The 2023 ones were a surge in burning and exports while Quebec's dams were low, which my province-by-province matching couldn't credit. A simpler detector (largest residual) *did* beat chance, though not the bar. So the honest takeaway is a hypothesis: monthly anomalies look like provinces swapping generation and trade. Adding events after seeing the flags would be fitting the answer key to the exam, so the failure stands, and next time outages and cross-border matching go in the plan *before* mining.
- **K4** failed. A 12% development gain didn't survive sealed data: slightly better in 2025, worse in 2026, when "same month last year" was unusually accurate. Even the best model in hindsight would have failed. So the deployed forecast is seasonal naive, and the fancier model is shown only as a challenger.

## Step 6: using the knowledge

The project ends with a small **Streamlit dashboard** (archetypes, change points by province, flagged months, a 12-month forecast) and a `refresh.py` that rebuilds everything from the next StatCan release with the *frozen* rules: nothing is re-tuned, so a refresh is monitoring, not a new experiment. Redistribution follows the Statistics Canada Open Licence, with an attribution file next to the app.

## The critic in the loop

Every step was reviewed by an independent Claude "critic" with the persona of a world authority on KDD, read-only access and permission to re-run numbers. After transformation it scored the work 7/10 with three major issues:
- my change-point test was close to self-confirming;
- my anomaly test couldn't fail, because the lift threshold never bound at a 12% base rate;
- my "pre-registration" had no verifiable timestamp.

Fixing those (trimmed-window stability, a margin-based persistence test, event-level precision with a permutation null, a hashed manifest of the rule files) is what made two of the four tests able to fail, and they did. After evaluation it scored 7.5/10 and caught two over-claims, the BC/PEI "declines" and my New Brunswick story, which I corrected in the notebook, the knowledge base and the dashboard.

## What I'd tell someone starting a KDD project
1. **Read the methodology notes of your source before trusting a trend.** The 2016 break would have produced a spectacular, fake "renewables boom".
2. **Write down how each finding could fail before you look.** Otherwise every pattern looks like knowledge.
3. **Report failures as knowledge.** "Monthly anomalies in power data are supply-side, not weather" and "12-month demand forecasts rarely beat last year's value" are useful findings.
